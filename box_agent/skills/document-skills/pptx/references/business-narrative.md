# Business Evidence and Storyline

Use this guide when planning product promotion, sales or enterprise-value decks.
Preserve supplied page order, source boundaries and explicit audiences. Apply the
enterprise questions only when business adoption is part of the requested story
or the product value being claimed; do not turn a consumer introduction into a
procurement proposal or a simple supplied brief into a research project.

## Research the decision, not just the product

Choose the primary reader and the next decision: try a task, approve a pilot,
compare suppliers, or evaluate deployment. When individual and enterprise uses
coexist, choose a lead path and make the other supporting context. An enterprise
promise needs enterprise evidence; user totals and a personal success story do
not establish a business outcome.

After broad discovery, identify which buyer questions remain unanswered. Keep a
compact coverage table in an existing research insight/cross-verification file:
`decision question | needed evidence | source/fact reference | status/next lookup | outline page`.
Start it before direct reads. Before each next read, select the highest-impact
open question for this audience and an observed source likely to answer it;
record that choice in `status/next lookup`. A failed case read leaves its question
open. Within the remaining budget and blocked-source rules, prefer another
accessible candidate for that task or condition over optional product history.
When the workflow includes content review, hold reserved reads until it returns,
then apply the same question priority; a failed candidate does not release the
reserve for background.
Read the actual pages; another summary of the same release adds no independent
support. Update references to verified fact IDs after research validation.
Prefer direct page text for the case and commercial terms at the center of
the decision. If either rests only on a search or reader summary, preserve that basis and
confidence instead of presenting it as a directly inspected document.

| Buyer question | Evidence to seek |
| --- | --- |
| Which business task is worth changing? | Workflow bottleneck, affected role, existing process and consequence; not just a large market forecast |
| What changes in that workflow? | Product mechanism, inputs, outputs, integrations and human review needed for that task |
| Has a comparable organization used it? | Customer or identifiable organizational case with task, deployment context and reported result |
| Why does the result matter commercially? | Supported change in turnaround, review effort, throughput, quality or service capacity; separate observed outcomes from proposed benefits |
| What is required to adopt it? | Current product/enterprise documentation, deployment and data boundaries, integration effort, licensing or public pricing where available |
| What should the buyer do next? | A next action justified by the evidence; when a trial is needed, measure the intended benefit as well as costs and constraints against the current workflow, and label proposed criteria as recommendations |

For a deck making enterprise claims, deliberately look for an enterprise case
and adoption/commercial evidence within the selected research budget. A broad
scan that finds only capabilities and adoption totals has not answered those
questions. Use the entity plus the uncovered task/customer/industry/result or
deployment/pricing intent, then follow promising customer, vendor or analyst
references to their exact pages. Check dates and whether a capability is shipped,
announced, in pilot, or only planned. Do not treat an old launch post as proof of
the current commercial offering.

Keep the research route's budget and blocked-source stopping rules. Prioritize
missing central claims over more brand-history material. If a material target
remains unavailable after bounded attempts, record what was tried and narrow
the claim or disclose the gap; do not manufacture a case, pricing, ROI or more
dimension files to suggest completeness. Fast production does not make the
initial discovery scan a complete business argument.
Distinguish `verified`, `attempted but unavailable`, `not checked within budget`
and `not relevant to this audience`; the last two do not mean information is not
public. Before outlining, resolve each material question to evidence or an honest
gap and put its destination page in the table. An identified source left unread
is still a gap. Do not silently drop verified commercial terms during drafting.
For a current product or service introduction, allocate discovery to a current
first-party offering/access page and its relevant plan/help links before spending
the budget on more historical announcements. A dated release establishes the
state at that date; it does not establish that an announced plan is still pending.
Reconcile that history with the current offering. If current status cannot be
checked, date the historical claim visibly and leave current availability unknown.
A corporate release or a search with no price result does not establish that
public plans are unavailable. Give a material adoption gap a targeted read within
the remaining budget before leaving it unresolved.

## Turn a case into evidence

Select the case closest to the primary reader's task and adoption decision
before allocating pages. Compare promising cases found during discovery by
task relevance, identifiable context, result and source strength; a familiar
customer name or a large percentage alone is not sufficient. Prefer the
customer's own account or an original study when available. An attributed vendor
case with a relevant organizational task, intervention and qualitative result can
answer the case question at that reported scope; ROI metrics are not required.
A practitioner account answers an organizational-task question only when its
source establishes that workflow context. Personal practice plus a count of
enterprise users leaves that question open; retain each as narrower evidence.
Match the case to the promise being sold: a distribution partnership or product
integration establishes reach or availability, not what a customer accomplished.
Before spending the reading budget on more background, target an unanswered
value claim with a task/outcome search. Compare the resulting candidates; do not
mark the business-proof question covered merely because a prominent partner has
an official case page. Source authority does not substitute for task relevance.
Write its task, intervention, deliverable, outcome and
conditions into the existing evidence ledger, with the organization or source's
anonymous description and source/date. Resolve its verified fact IDs before
writing the case page. Preserve source wording and use declared entity aliases;
if validation rejects a record, inspect the source and correct the record or
alias instead of silently replacing a supported case with a logo list. Do not
invent a customer identity or a missing outcome to complete the record.
For an anonymous source, a neutral title such as `匿名案例` avoids suggesting a
named customer project; describe the sector and task in the visible body.
A customer logo, partner list or count of enterprise users is adoption context,
not a completed case. A consumer or public-interest example can supplement an
enterprise story but cannot substitute for its business proof.
In the case page's visible copy, use the source's concrete input, changed steps,
deliverable and human review. Let those details replace generic capability
bullets already covered elsewhere. Keep omitted steps or outcomes unknown;
a metric card alone is not a complete account.

Keep attribution visible: a vendor-reported case or a vendor's account of an
analyst report is not an independently audited result. Separate production use,
pilot and proposed demonstration. Keep the source's workload, time period,
population and units. Do not convert vague "days to hours" into exact staffing,
percent savings or ROI. A second case earns space when it proves a different
decision-relevant claim, not merely because it names another industry.

## Build the commercial argument

Connect **business task → changed work → supported result → buyer implication**.
For example, a shorter document-preparation cycle may support quicker review;
it does not establish higher revenue or headcount savings without that evidence.
Distinguish product capability, reported case outcome and the deck's inference.
Put important conditions next to the visible claim, not only in speaker notes.
Explain why the supported result matters to this reader, and what it does not
establish. Compare a proposed change with the current workflow or a relevant
alternative on the same task and units when evidence permits; avoid invented
competitor weaknesses or an automatic "adopt" recommendation. Trial, purchase,
retain the current approach and defer are all valid conclusions. Let the user's
purpose and the evidence choose the outcome.

Address adoption friction as part of value: data access, integration, permissions,
human review, training and commercial terms as relevant. Use current public terms
with date, edition, currency and billing basis; separate monthly rates, annual
commitments, per-seat fees and usage limits. Keep regional/version differences
explicit. Map the proposed workflow to its chargeable components, including
included allowances and what happens when they run out. A feature listed as
"included" does not by itself establish unlimited use: follow the applicable
usage/credit terms for the core task's allowance, overage authorization and
exhaustion behavior. One optional add-on's
price does not establish the cost of the core workflow; distinguish optional
usage from charges or limits that affect the proposed use. In the existing
question map, give each cost-driving part of the proposed task its own inclusion,
allowance and exhaustion question, or a specific unresolved condition. Do not
mark total-cost coverage complete after finding only a seat price or add-on fee. Explain which task
need or constraint makes an edition or deployment choice useful; a price table
alone does not establish a reason to buy. List unpriced
integration, infrastructure and review effort as costs
to measure, not zero costs. When cost or outcome data are absent, use a
clearly proposed pilot measurement plan instead of invented financial returns:
measure the same task's baseline and assisted effort, accepted-output quality,
review/rework and deployment effort before deciding whether to expand. Do not
turn deployment support into a security/compliance guarantee.

Market size, brand history and user scale earn pages only when they answer the
chosen decision. Neither a large market nor a popular product proves that this
buyer will benefit. A useful story can move directly from the task problem to a
mechanism, a relevant case, adoption conditions and a concrete next decision.
Respect explicit page-count constraints; otherwise let distinct contributions
determine length, without adding a section for every research file.

Apply the coverage and contribution review in `outline.md` before design. Give
each core business claim a primary page. Other pages should develop its case,
conditions or implications rather than repeat its feature list. Release history,
platform lists and market scale earn space when they affect the reader's
decision or the requested scope. Preserve distinct industry applications and
alternatives when they answer that scope; deduplication is not a reason to
discard them. The final action must follow from what the deck demonstrated.
Make that action executable: include an observed trial/download/contact entry
or a supplied internal owner/channel, as appropriate to the audience. A sequence
of evaluation steps alone does not tell a new reader where to begin. Do not
invent contact details or present an unvisited destination as verified.
